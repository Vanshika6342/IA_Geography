\subsubsection{Velocity}
	
	\begin{figure}[H]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{Images/AverageVelocity.png}
		\caption{Average Velocity at Each Site}
	\end{figure}

The velocity of a river, according to the Bradshaw model, should increase further downstream due to the predicted widening of the river channel and increase in depth. The river Modau on the other hand, had a decrease at the very start from 0.5725 m/s at Site 1 to 0.24 m/s at Site 2. Site 3 shows a high spike at 1.1 m/s, however following this the velocity decreases again. Only Site 5 to Site 7 embody the characteristics of the Bradshaw Model. This portrays that the velocity of the river does not conform to the Bradshaw Model, shown by the negative value of the line of best fit.

The reason for the very high value at Site 3 is as the channel had been straightened, with mattressing on the bottom due to the channel's location in a town, with a footpath had been made alongside it.

\subsubsection{Channel Efficiency}

% Channel Efficiency
\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
		\includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{Images/ChannelEfficiencyGraph.png}
	\caption{Channel Efficiency at Different Sites}
\end{figure}

Channel Efficiency, calculated by dividing the cross-sectional area by the wetted perimeter, is said to increase as one moves downstream according to the Bradshaw Model. This is as when depth and width increase, cross-sectional area and wetted perimeter increase. As shown in the graph above channel efficiency fluctuates at the start as there is a sudden, sharp increase from Site 1 at 14.4\% to 25.3\% at Site 2. The maximum is seen at Site 3 where it reaches a high of 26.6\%, however, it drops after this till 19\%. From Site 6 to 8, a steady increase is seen. Overall, there is an increasing trend, albeit a very small increase as shown by the line of best fit, but one can see that the channel efficiency corresponds to the Bradshaw Model.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{Images/VeloChan.png}
	\caption{Channel Efficiency and velocity correlation across different sites.}
\end{figure}

The Bradshaw Model states that both channel efficiency and velocity increase downstream, however, only channel efficiency conforms to the Bradshaw Model. The values of both data sets are mainly similar at sites 2, 5, 6, but this doesn't mean they follow the same trend. There values only get close to each other here. One can see that despite channel efficiency increasing from site 1 to site 3, velocity fluctuates till Site 3 where it suddenly increases to 1 m/s, its highest speed. 

The only similarity is that they both have their maximum values at Site 3, however channel efficiency is at its lowest at Site 1 with 14.4\% and velocity is lowest at Site 8 with 0.0333 m/s. This is as Site 8 was the mouth of the river, causing the water to slow because of sedimentation and delta formation. Overall, the datasets show us that the hypothesis partially corresponds to the Bradshaw Model as channel efficiency's overall trend follows the model, however velocity does not. Additionally, the hypothesis is not supported as there is a very weak negative correlation between velocity and channel efficiency with a correlation coefficient (Spearman's Rank) of -0.0238 (3 s.f.).

\subsection{Second Hypothesis}
\label{H2}

\subsubsection{Channel Width and Depth}

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{Images/AverageWidth.png}
	\caption{Average Channel Width at Each Site}
	\label{fig:AvgWidth}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{Images/AverageDepth.png}
	\caption{Average Channel Depth at Each Site}
	\label{fig:AvgDepth}
\end{figure}

The aforementioned Bradshaw Model dictates, as stated in the analysis of the previous hypothesis, that the average depth and width of a river channel increases downstream to the erosional processes present in the river and its growing prominence as one approaches the mouth of the river. As shown in the figures above, the Modau river channel's width has a positive rate of change of 0.2298, as does the channel's depth with 0.0045. This shows that river depth and width follow the predicted characteristics of the Bradshaw Model. It is to be noted, however, that there is a greater increase in the width of the river channel than its depth. 

There are anomalies present for the width at Site 6 and 7 as it drops from approximately 7 meters at Site 5 to 5.613m at Site 6 and then further down to 3.873m at Site 7. For the average depth, there is a very stark anomaly present at Site 3, with a depth of 0.417m, where it suddenly shoots up from 0.179m at Site 2, but then afterwards drops down abruptly to 0.18m at Site 4.

\subsubsection{Discharge}

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{Images/RiverDischarge.png}
	\caption{Graph of River Discharge.}
\end{figure}

In accordance with the Bradshaw Model, the river discharge is said to increase as channel efficiency increases. In the Modau River, there is an increase seen from Site 1, 0.168 cumecs, to Site 3, 2.04 cumecs, however, after then there is a steady decline till Site 8. This does not conform to the predictions of the Model.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{Images/RivDisOnDepthNWidth.png}
	\caption{Graph of River Discharge in relation to Depth and Width of the channel at each site.}

Shown above is the graph of the River Discharge in relation to the average channel depth and channel width of the Modau River. As portrayed in the graph above, there is no proper correlation in the graph above, as despite the increasing trend of the river's average depth and width the river discharge has an overall decrease as seen by the line of best fit, with a negative rate of change of 0.078 as shown in \Cref{fig:RivDisNDepth}. Nevertheless, one can see that the river discharge has a moderate to high correlation of 0.595, with the width of the river channel, which is better seen in \Cref{fig:RivDisNDepth}, compared to the its weak, but positive, correlation of 0.333 with the average channel depth.


\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{Images/RivDisOnDepth.png}
	\caption{A closer look at River Discharge, but mainly in relation to Depth.}
	\label{fig:RivDisNDepth}

One of reasons for the negative trend of channel efficiency is due to the velocity having an overall decrease as well due to many hard- and soft-engineering techniques. Channel efficiency has an overall decrease due to placements of rip-rap, meanders, and more throughout the middle course and natural sedimentation at the mouth of the river. Nevertheless, this shows us that the data sets don't fully follow the predictions of the Bradshaw Model, and has only a weak to moderate correlation with the depth and width of the river channel. It partially supports the hypothesis as both channel width and channel depth conform to the Bradshaw model, however channel efficiency doesn't.


\subsection{Hypothesis 3}
\label{H3}

% Bedload Size
	\begin{figure}[H]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{Images/GENew.jpg}
		\caption{Bedload Size at Different Sites}
		\end{figure}
		
In accordance with the Bradshaw model, bedload size has to decrease as one moves downstream due to the increased amount and impact of erosional processes, especially attrition, corrosion, corrasion, and traction. In the data map above, one can see the clear decreasing trend of the bedload size. There are two anomalies present at Site 6, where the bedload size increases from 38.3 mm at Site 5 to 43.9 mm at Site 6. This is however, justified, as due to a fork in the river at Site 6, with little branches and stones caught at the small nick-point, the flow of the river was slowed down in comparison to Site 5, which had a straight channel with mattressing which would instead increase the velocity, causing the erosional processes occurring to be more influential in breaking down the size of the bedload. 

This data-set completely supports the hypothesis that bedload size decreases by increased distance away from the source, which in turn also supports the Bradshaw Model.
